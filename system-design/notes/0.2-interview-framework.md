title: "0.2 The system design interview framework"
---

The system design round is a **structured conversation**, not a quiz. The interviewer
wants to see how you take a vague, open-ended prompt ("design a notification system")
and drive it to a concrete architecture while reasoning out loud about trade-offs. A
strong candidate is predictable: they follow a repeatable process, keep the interviewer
oriented, and never go silent.

This note is the process. Every case study in Part 9 and every Bloomberg question in
Part 10 is worked in exactly these steps.

## What the round actually measures

- **Problem framing.** Can you turn an ambiguous ask into explicit requirements and a
  bounded scope?
- **Structured thinking.** Do you move top-down (requirements to high-level design to
  details) instead of jumping straight to "use Kafka"?
- **Breadth.** Do you know the standard building blocks (load balancers, caches,
  queues, replication, sharding) and when each applies?
- **Depth.** Can you go two or three levels deep on at least one component when pushed?
- **Trade-off reasoning.** Every choice has a cost. Can you name it and justify the
  choice against the requirements?
- **Communication.** Do you keep the interviewer with you, respond to hints, and manage
  your own time?

## The framework

![The seven phases of a system design round](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-2-framework-flow.png)

1. **Requirements and scope** - functional and non-functional, plus what is explicitly
   out of scope.
2. **Back-of-the-envelope estimation** - scale numbers that will drive the design.
3. **API design** - the contract between client and system.
4. **Data model** - core entities and how they are accessed.
5. **High-level design** - boxes and arrows; the end-to-end request path.
6. **Deep dives** - pick two or three components and go deep.
7. **Bottlenecks and trade-offs** - find the weak points, then defend or fix them.

The arrows are not strictly one-way. When a deep dive reveals a problem, you go back and
adjust the high-level design. Say that out loud when you do it - iterating is a positive
signal.

### Time budget

A typical round is 35 to 50 minutes. Do not spend 20 minutes on requirements. A rough
allocation for a 45-minute round:

![Rough time budget for a 45-minute round](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-2-phases-timeline.png)

Watch the clock yourself. If you are 20 minutes in and still clarifying requirements,
the interviewer cannot assess your design skills.

---

## Phase 1 - Requirements and scope (about 6 min)

Never start drawing. Spend the first few minutes turning the prompt into a written list
of requirements on the board. This is the single highest-leverage part of the round: a
good scope makes the rest easy, a bad scope makes it impossible.

![Functional vs non-functional requirements](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-2-requirements-tree.png)

### Functional requirements - what the system does

List the two to four core user actions. For "design Twitter": post a tweet, follow a
user, view a home timeline. Explicitly **defer** everything else (search, DMs,
trending, ads) - say "I'll leave these out unless you want me to cover them." Narrowing
scope is a strength, not a weakness.

### Non-functional requirements - how well it does it

These shape the architecture far more than the feature list. Pin down numbers:

| Dimension | Questions to ask | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Scale | How many users (DAU/MAU)? QPS at peak? Data volume? Growth rate? | Decides single-box vs distributed, sharding, caching |
| Latency | p50 and p99 targets for the key operations? | Push vs pull, caching, precomputation, data locality |
| Availability | How many nines? Is downtime acceptable for writes? Reads? | Replication, failover, multi-region, CP vs AP |
| Consistency | Must a read always see the latest write? Or is stale data OK for a few seconds? | Strong vs eventual consistency, quorum design |
| Durability | Is it catastrophic to lose a recent write? | Replication factor, sync vs async, WAL, acks |
| Read/write ratio | Read-heavy (100:1) or write-heavy? | Where to cache, whether to fan-out on write or read |
| Access pattern | Point lookups, range scans, full-text, analytics? | Choice of data store and index |
| Cost and compliance | Budget sensitivity? Data residency, retention, PII? | Storage tiering, region strategy |

If the interviewer says "you decide," state an assumption ("I'll assume 10M DAU, 100:1
read-heavy, p99 under 200ms") and move on. Write assumptions on the board so you can
revisit them.

### Output of this phase

A short, visible list: functional requirements, non-functional requirements with
numbers, and out-of-scope items. Confirm it with the interviewer before moving on.

---

## Phase 2 - Back-of-the-envelope estimation (about 4 min)

Convert the scale requirements into the numbers that will justify design decisions:

- **QPS**: average and peak (peak is usually 2 to 10x average). `writes/sec`,
  `reads/sec`.
- **Storage**: per-record size x records/day x retention. Project 1 and 5 years out.
- **Bandwidth**: QPS x payload size, in and out.
- **Memory for cache**: apply the 80/20 rule - can the hot 20% of data fit in RAM?

You do not need precision. You need the right order of magnitude and a clear method.
Full technique and the latency-numbers cheat sheet are in note 0.3. Keep the arithmetic
simple: round to powers of ten, use 100K seconds per day, 1M seconds per fortnight.

Then state the implication: "~50K write QPS and 40 TB/year means this will not fit on
one machine - we need partitioning and a replicated store."

---

## Phase 3 - API design (about 4 min)

Define the interface before the internals. It forces you to nail down exactly what the
system offers and what data crosses the boundary.

- One endpoint per functional requirement.
- Show the method, path, key parameters, and response shape.
- Note **pagination** (cursor over offset), **auth** (who is the caller), and
  **idempotency** for writes (client-supplied key so retries are safe).

```
POST /v1/tweets            { text, media_ids? }            -> { tweet_id, created_at }
GET  /v1/timeline?cursor=  &limit=20                        -> { tweets[], next_cursor }
POST /v1/follows           { followee_id }                  -> 200
```

Mention whether the transport is REST, gRPC, or a WebSocket/SSE stream, and why. For
low-latency internal service-to-service calls, gRPC. For a browser receiving live
updates, WebSocket or SSE. (See Part 2.)

---

## Phase 4 - Data model (about 4 min)

List the core entities, their key fields, and the relationships. Then, for each major
read and write, state the **access pattern** and let it drive the store choice:

- Point lookups by key, high write volume, simple schema -> key-value or wide-column
  (DynamoDB, Cassandra).
- Relational integrity, ad-hoc queries, transactions -> relational (Postgres, MySQL).
- Full-text search -> a search index (Elasticsearch) alongside the primary store.
- Large binary objects -> blob storage (S3), with only metadata in the database.

Call out the **primary key / partition key** for each table - it determines how the
data shards and whether your hot queries stay within one partition.

---

## Phase 5 - High-level design (about 12 min)

Now draw. Put the client on the left, walk the request left to right through every
component, and label each arrow with what flows over it. A generic template to adapt:

![A generic high-level design template](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-2-hld-template.png)

Standard building blocks and when to add each:

- **DNS / GeoDNS** - route users to the nearest region.
- **CDN** - serve static assets and cacheable responses from the edge.
- **Load balancer** - spread traffic across app servers; health-check and remove dead
  ones.
- **API gateway** - authentication, rate limiting, routing, request shaping (optional
  but common).
- **Stateless app servers** - hold no session state, so you scale them horizontally by
  just adding machines. Push state into the data tier.
- **Cache** - Redis/Memcached in front of the database for hot reads.
- **Primary database + read replicas** - replicas absorb read load; primary takes
  writes.
- **Message queue + async workers** - move slow or spiky work (fan-out, notifications,
  media processing, analytics) off the request path.
- **Blob storage** - images, video, files, backups.

Trace one concrete request end to end ("a user opens their timeline") and one write
("a user posts a tweet"). Show where the write fans out, what is synchronous vs
asynchronous, and where a cache is read or invalidated.

---

## Phase 6 - Deep dives (about 12 min)

The interviewer will steer here, or you should offer: "The interesting part is the
timeline fan-out - can I go deep on that?" Pick the two or three components where the
requirements create real difficulty. Common deep-dive axes:

- **Scaling a tier** - sharding scheme, choosing a partition key, avoiding hot
  partitions, rebalancing (Part 3.4).
- **Data storage** - schema, index design, read vs write amplification, replication and
  consistency (Part 3).
- **Caching** - what to cache, invalidation strategy, stampede protection, consistency
  with the source of truth (Part 4).
- **A specific hard sub-problem** - the timeline fan-out, the notification fan-out, the
  top-K aggregation, the search index, the real-time delivery layer.
- **Consistency and correctness** - what anomalies are possible, and are they
  acceptable given the requirements.

Depth means: name the concrete mechanism, its failure mode, and how you handle the
failure. "Consistent hashing with 150 virtual nodes per physical node; when a node dies
its keys spread across the remaining nodes; we replicate each key to the next two nodes
on the ring so a death loses no data."

---

## Phase 7 - Bottlenecks and trade-offs (about 3 min)

Close by attacking your own design before the interviewer does:

- **Single points of failure** - every component: is it replicated? What happens when
  it dies? How does the system recover?
- **Hot spots** - a celebrity user, a viral item, a popular key, a thundering herd on
  cache expiry.
- **Scaling limits** - which tier breaks first at 10x traffic, and what is the next
  move?
- **Consistency gaps** - where can a client see stale or out-of-order data, and is that
  within the requirements?
- **Cost** - the expensive part of the design, and a cheaper alternative if budget
  mattered.

State the main trade-off you made and why it fits the requirements: "I chose eventual
consistency for the timeline because the requirement allows a few seconds of staleness
and it lets reads scale off replicas without cross-shard coordination."

---

## Communicating and driving the round

- **Think out loud, always.** Silence gives the interviewer nothing to evaluate.
- **Keep them oriented.** "We've scoped it and estimated scale. Next I'll design the
  API, then the data model, then the high-level architecture."
- **Take hints.** If they ask "what happens when that server crashes?" they are telling
  you to design for it now. Do not defend the gap - close it.
- **State assumptions and move on.** Do not wait for permission on every detail.
- **Manage your time.** If a deep dive is running long, say "I could go further here but
  let me make sure we cover the storage layer too."
- **It is a dialogue.** Ask "does that match what you had in mind?" at phase boundaries.

## Common mistakes

- Jumping to a solution before scoping the problem.
- Over-scoping - trying to build every feature instead of the core two or three.
- Naming technologies without justification ("we'll use Cassandra" - why?).
- Staying entirely at the high level and never going deep on anything.
- Going silent while thinking.
- Ignoring failure and operations entirely.
- Not doing the estimation, so design choices have no numeric basis.
- Losing track of time and never reaching the interesting part.

## Watch a senior run the framework - "design a URL shortener"

Here is the whole framework at speed, in the voice you should be using. Forty minutes,
narrated.

**Phase 1 - requirements (about 4 min).** "Let me scope this. Core functions: one,
shorten a long URL into a short code; two, redirect a short code to the original. I'll
also assume: custom aliases are optional, link expiration is optional, and per-link
click analytics is a nice-to-have I'll design for but not deep-dive. Out of scope: user
accounts, editing, a dashboard UI.

Non-functional: this is extremely **read-heavy** - people click links far more than they
create them, I'll assume 100:1. Redirect latency has to be low, it's on the user's
critical path - target p99 under 50 ms. Availability matters more for redirects than for
creation; a failed redirect is a broken link on someone's site. Short codes must be
unique and ideally not guessable in sequence. Scale - let me put numbers on it."

**Phase 2 - estimation (about 3 min).** "Say 100M new links per day. That's `100M / 1e5
= 1,000 writes/sec`, call it 3,000 at peak. Reads at 100:1 are 100,000/sec average,
~300,000/sec peak. Storage: each row is a short code, the long URL, an owner id, a
timestamp - round to 500 bytes, so `100M x 500 B = 50 GB/day`, about 18 TB over a year
with retention, times a replication factor of 3. That's small enough that the working
set could sit entirely in cache. **Implication:** the redirect path must be served from
memory, not disk; the write path just needs a fast unique-ID generator; the store can be
a simple sharded key-value store."

**Phase 3 - API (about 3 min).**

```
POST /v1/urls      { long_url, custom_alias? }   -> { short_code, short_url }
GET  /{short_code}                                -> 301 redirect to long_url
GET  /v1/urls/{short_code}/stats                  -> { clicks, ... }
```

"POST is idempotent per (owner, long_url) so a retry returns the same code. The redirect
returns 301 permanent so browsers and proxies cache it - fewer hits on us - though I
lose per-click analytics accuracy that way, so if analytics is important I'd use 302
instead. That's a trade-off I'd confirm with you."

**Phase 4 - data model (about 3 min).** "One main table: `short_code` (primary key,
this is what I shard on), `long_url`, `owner_id`, `created_at`, `expires_at`. Point
lookups by `short_code` only - no range scans, no joins - so a key-value or wide-column
store is the right fit, not a relational DB. Analytics is a separate append-only store
keyed by `short_code` plus time bucket."

**Phase 5 - high-level design (about 12 min).**

![Worked high-level design for a URL shortener](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-2-worked-urlshortener.png)

"Client hits a load balancer, which fronts two stateless services - a write service and
a redirect service - so I can scale the read path independently of the write path.

Write path: the write service calls an **ID generator** to get a new unique integer,
encodes it in base62 to get a ~7-character code, writes the row to the KV store, returns
the short URL.

Redirect path: the redirect service looks up the code in **Redis** first. On a hit -
which is almost always, given the tiny working set - it returns a 301 immediately. On a
miss it reads from the KV store, backfills the cache, and redirects. It also emits a
click event onto a **queue**; an analytics worker consumes that asynchronously so
analytics never slows the redirect."

**Phase 6 - deep dive: the ID generator (about 9 min).** "This is the interesting part -
generating unique, non-sequential codes at 3,000 writes/sec across many machines without
collisions.

Option A: a single auto-increment counter in a database. Simple, guarantees uniqueness,
but it's a single point of failure and a write bottleneck.

Option B - what I'd choose: **counter ranges**. A coordination service (ZooKeeper/etcd,
or just a row in the DB) hands each write-service instance a block of 10,000 IDs at a
time. The instance serves IDs from its block in memory and asks for a new block when it
runs low. Now ID generation is a local operation - no coordination on the hot path - and
if an instance dies we just lose its remaining block, which is fine, IDs are cheap.

The codes are still roughly sequential, which the requirement said to avoid. Two fixes:
encode the ID with a per-instance permutation, or just accept it - I'd ask you how much
enumeration resistance actually matters here.

Collision handling if I used random codes instead: generate, attempt an insert with a
uniqueness constraint, retry on conflict. At 7 base62 chars that's 3.5 trillion codes,
so conflicts stay rare until the keyspace is very full."

**Phase 7 - bottlenecks (about 3 min).** "Single points of failure: the load balancer
needs a standby; Redis should be a replicated cluster, and note that a cold cache after
a full Redis failure would dump ~300k QPS onto the KV store - I'd mitigate with request
coalescing and by warming the cache. The KV store is sharded by `short_code` with RF=3,
so a node loss is survivable. Hot keys: a viral link could hammer one cache node - I'd
replicate hot entries across nodes or push them to the CDN. The queue can buffer if the
analytics worker falls behind; if the queue itself fills, I drop analytics events before
I ever affect redirects. Main trade-off: I chose 301 redirects for cache-friendliness
and accepted less precise click counts, because the core requirement is fast reliable
redirects, not analytics."

That is a complete round. Notice the pattern: every phase ends by stating what the
numbers or the choice *imply* for the next phase, and every component comes with its
failure mode.

## Bloomberg-specific notes

Bloomberg system design rounds (see 10.0) tend to:

- Be run on **paper or a whiteboard**, often with **no coding**, as a back-and-forth
  discussion.
- Fire **rapid follow-up questions** and deliberate curveballs ("what if this service
  isn't available?", "how do these snapshots get stored?", "your servers just failed -
  now what?"). Body language may not signal whether you are on track. Treat tricky
  questions as a probe of your fundamentals, not as proof you are wrong.
- Emphasize **real-time data, low latency, reliability, and no data loss** - financial
  data context. Expect questions about exactly-once processing, ordering, retention of
  ever-growing data, and recovery after a server or service failure.
- Reward candidates who **name the right primitive**: an event-listener / telemetry
  service for capturing client activity, a message queue for decoupling producers and
  consumers, snapshots plus incremental updates for late joiners, idempotency for safe
  retries.

Have a default flow ready so you can start fast, and be prepared to switch between
high-level and low-level design on request.

## One-page checklist

- [ ] Functional requirements: the core 2 to 4 actions. Everything else out of scope.
- [ ] Non-functional: scale, latency (p50/p99), availability, consistency, durability,
  read/write ratio - with numbers.
- [ ] Estimation: peak QPS, storage per year, bandwidth, cache size. State the
  implication.
- [ ] API: one endpoint per requirement; pagination, auth, idempotency.
- [ ] Data model: entities, keys, access patterns -> store choice per entity.
- [ ] High-level design: client to storage, every arrow labeled; trace one read and one
  write.
- [ ] Deep dives: 2 to 3 components; mechanism, failure mode, recovery.
- [ ] Bottlenecks: SPOFs, hot spots, 10x traffic, consistency gaps, cost.
- [ ] Trade-offs: state the big one and tie it back to a requirement.
