---
title: "1.1 Functional vs non-functional requirements"
---

0.2's interview framework already named this split as phase one of every round. This note
is the vocabulary underneath it - why the split exists, why non-functional requirements are
the ones that actually decide the architecture, and the fuller taxonomy that Part 1's
remaining notes each dig into one piece of.

## What vs. how well

![The fundamental split: what the system does vs. how well it does it](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/1-1-what-vs-how-well.png)

**Functional requirements** describe *what* the system does - the user-facing actions and
behaviors: "a user can post a photo," "a user can search by tag," "an admin can delete a
post." These are usually the easy part of a requirements conversation: they're explicit,
nameable, and - critically - **negotiable**. Scope can always shrink ("I'll leave comments
out of this design unless you want me to cover them," per 0.2's phase 1) without changing
what kind of system you're building.

**Non-functional requirements** describe *how well*, or under what **constraints**, the
system does those things - how many users, how fast, how available, how consistent, how
durable, how much it costs. These are the hard part, for a specific reason: they're usually
**implicit**. Nobody says "and also, p99 latency needs to be under 200ms" in casual
conversation about a feature - that number has to be actively elicited (0.2's "if the
interviewer says 'you decide,' state an assumption out loud") or the system gets built
against an unstated, guessed-at target that may be wrong by orders of magnitude.

## The same feature, two completely different systems

![Identical functional requirement, different architectures, purely from non-functional numbers](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/1-1-same-function-different-arch.png)

This is the concrete reason the split matters as much as it does: **the functional
requirement alone never determines the architecture.** "A user can search for a product" is
satisfied equally by a single Postgres instance with a B-tree index (3.3) serving 10,000
products at 100 QPS, and by a sharded search index (3.4) behind aggressive caching (4.1-4.3)
and a CDN (4.4) serving 500 million products at 200,000 QPS with a 50ms p99 target. Same
sentence, completely different systems - every architectural decision in this entire
course is, underneath, a response to a *non-functional* number, not a functional one. This
is exactly why 0.3's back-of-the-envelope estimation phase comes immediately after
requirements and before any box gets drawn: the numbers from this phase are what the rest
of the design actually has to satisfy.

## The non-functional taxonomy

0.2 already gives the practical, interview-ready version of this - a table of the
dimensions worth asking about and why. The fuller picture, and where each dimension gets
its own real vocabulary in this course:

![Each non-functional category gets its own dedicated note in Part 1 (or elsewhere)](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/1-1-nfr-taxonomy.png)

- **Scalability and performance** - how the system behaves as load grows, and the
  latency-vs-throughput distinction that governs most capacity planning - 1.3.
- **Availability and reliability** - how much uptime, and whether "up" also means
  "answering correctly" - a sharper distinction than it first sounds - 1.2.
- **Consistency** - must a read always reflect the most recent write, or is some staleness
  acceptable - 1.5, with the CAP/PACELC trade-off that governs the choice in 1.4.
- **Durability** - can a committed write ever be lost - 1.2 for the definition, 2.7 for the
  actual recovery mechanics.
- **Security** - authentication, authorization, encryption, threat surface - Part 7.
- **Maintainability** - how easy the system is to understand, change, and operate over its
  lifetime, independent of how it performs on day one - 1.3.
- **Cost** - infrastructure spend per user or per request, and whether the design fits a
  stated budget - 1.3 and 8.3.
- **Compliance** - data residency, retention policy, PII handling - called out per case
  study where it's relevant rather than given its own dedicated note, since it's often
  domain- and jurisdiction-specific rather than a general system-design pattern.

## Why getting this wrong is the common failure mode

A system design round (and a real design document) most often goes wrong not because the
functional requirements were misunderstood, but because a non-functional one was **assumed
rather than stated** - silently designing for "normal" scale when the actual target was
100x that, or silently assuming strong consistency was fine when the product actually
needed to survive a multi-region partition. The fix is procedural, not clever: state every
non-functional assumption **out loud**, with a number attached, before designing around it
- exactly 0.2's phase 1 discipline, and the reason this entire Part exists as its own
section of the course rather than being folded into "estimation" as an afterthought.

## Cheat sheet

```
FUNCTIONAL    WHAT the system does - explicit, user-facing, NEGOTIABLE (scope
              can shrink without changing the kind of system being built).

NON-          HOW WELL / under what CONSTRAINTS - usually IMPLICIT, has to be
FUNCTIONAL    actively elicited with a number attached, and is what actually
              determines the architecture. The SAME functional requirement
              ("search for a product") can be satisfied by a single Postgres
              instance or a sharded, cached, CDN-backed search system -
              purely depending on the non-functional numbers attached to it.

TAXONOMY      scalability/performance (1.3) - availability/reliability (1.2)
              - consistency (1.5) - durability (1.2, 2.7) - security (Part 7)
              - maintainability (1.3) - cost (1.3, 8.3) - compliance
              (per case study).

COMMON        a non-functional requirement ASSUMED rather than STATED is the
FAILURE       most common root cause of a design that quietly targets the
MODE          wrong scale/consistency/availability. Fix: state every
              assumption out loud, with a number, before designing around it.
```
