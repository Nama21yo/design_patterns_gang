## The mindset shift

If you are coming from LeetCode, system design feels wrong at first. There is no
"Accepted." No hidden test cases. The interviewer hands you one sentence - "design a
notification system" - and then mostly listens.

Here is what is actually going on. The interviewer already knows a dozen good designs
for that problem. They are not checking whether you arrive at *the* answer, because
there isn't one. They are watching **how you think**:

- Do you pin down what you are actually building before you build it?
- Do you know the standard parts - load balancers, caches, queues, replicas, shards -
  and reach for the right one?
- When they push on a weak spot ("what happens when that server dies?"), do you close
  the gap calmly, or freeze?
- Can you say *why* you chose X over Y, in terms of the requirements?

So the unit of practice is not "solve the problem." It is "run the conversation." That
is why note 0.2 (the framework) is the spine of everything here - you are drilling a
repeatable process until it is automatic, the same way you drilled binary search.

A useful reframe: **you are the senior engineer in a design review, and the interviewer
is a skeptical teammate.** Your job is to propose an architecture, walk them through the
request path, and defend the trade-offs. If you can narrate a design to a colleague at a
whiteboard, you can pass this round.

## What "good" looks like

You do not need to design Google. For an early-career loop (which is what Bloomberg's
new-grad and SDE-1/2 system design rounds are), a strong performance is:

- Requirements and scope nailed down in the first 5 minutes, with numbers.
- A clean high-level design where every box has a reason and every arrow is labeled.
- One or two components taken two levels deep - real mechanisms, not just names.
- Failure handled: "this is a single point of failure, here is how I replicate it."
- Trade-offs stated out loud and tied back to the requirements.
- You drove the conversation and never went silent.

What you are *not* expected to do: recite paper details, know exact config flags, or
invent novel algorithms. Breadth with the confidence to go deep on demand beats
memorized depth everywhere.

## How this syllabus is built

The parts build on each other. You cannot reason about storage trade-offs without
consistency models; you cannot design a feed without caching and queues. This is the
dependency order:

![How the parts build on each other](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-1-dependency-map.png)

- **Part 0** - the framework and the estimation toolkit. Everything else assumes it.
- **Parts 1 to 7** - the building blocks, roughly in dependency order.
- **Part 8** - product thinking; how to turn a fuzzy ask into a spec. Read it anytime
  after Part 1.
- **Part 9** - twelve worked case studies. This is where the building blocks turn into
  designs.
- **Part 10** - the target: real Bloomberg questions, one per page.
- **Part 11** - papers and blogs, referenced from the notes as you go.

The dashed arrow matters: **pull Bloomberg questions forward.** As soon as you have
covered the prerequisites for a question, do it. Do not wait until you have finished all
of Parts 1 to 9.

## How to study one note

Passive reading does almost nothing for this skill. For each note:

1. **Read it once**, following the diagrams.
2. **Close it and redraw the main diagram from memory** on paper or a whiteboard. If you
   cannot, you did not understand it - reread that section.
3. **Say the trade-off out loud.** "You would pick a B-tree when reads dominate and you
   need range scans; an LSM-tree when writes dominate and you can tolerate read
   amplification." If you cannot say it in one breath, it is not yours yet.
4. **Write three flashcards** - not definitions, but decisions. Front: "Read-heavy feed,
   1M QPS, staleness OK for 30s - how do you serve it?" Back: the answer.
5. **Move on.** Do not polish. You will revisit the concept when it shows up in a case
   study, and that is when it sticks.

The case studies (Part 9) and Bloomberg questions (Part 10) are different: those you
**do**, not read. Timer on, talk out loud, draw the whole thing, *then* compare to the
write-up.

## The practice loop

Once you are into Part 9, most of your time should look like this:

![The practice loop](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-1-practice-loop.png)

- **Solve solo, out loud, 40 minutes, one timer.** Out loud is not optional - the round
  is verbal, and thinking silently is a different skill from narrating.
- **Record yourself** (phone audio is enough) and photograph the board. You will hear
  the filler, the backtracking, and the places you went quiet.
- **Compare to a reference**, then write down exactly three gaps. Common ones: missed a
  non-functional requirement, named a technology without justifying it, stayed
  high-level and never went deep, ignored failure.
- **Redo only the weak part** the next day. You do not need to redo the whole problem.

Do at least **four live mock interviews** with another person before the real thing -
Pramp, interviewing.io, a friend, anyone who will push back. Ideally at least one with
someone who has interviewed at Bloomberg. The body-language uncertainty and rapid
follow-ups (see 10.0) are hard to simulate alone.

## The study plan

This is about nine weeks at a steady pace. Compress it if you have less time (the triage
section below) or stretch it if you have more.

![An approximately nine-week plan](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Nama21yo/design_patterns_gang/main/system-design/images/0-1-study-plan.png)

| Week | Focus | Deliverable by end of week |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Part 0. Framework + estimation, cold. | Can run all 7 phases from memory; can do a Twitter-scale estimate in 5 min. |
| 2 | Part 1 fundamentals; start Part 2. | Can explain CAP, PACELC, and 4 consistency models with examples. |
| 3 | Part 2 communication; start Part 3. Start one case study. | Redis vs Kafka vs gRPC: when each. First full case study done + reviewed. |
| 4 | Part 3 storage (the big one). **Mock 1.** | Sharding, replication, quorums, consensus at a whiteboard level. |
| 5 | Part 4 caching; Part 5 messaging/streaming. | Cache strategies + invalidation; delivery semantics; fan-out on write vs read. |
| 6 | Parts 6 to 7 infra, security, time. **Mock 2.** Start Part 10. | Rate limiting, observability, logical clocks. 2 Bloomberg questions done. |
| 7 | Part 8; finish core case studies. | 6+ case studies done and reviewed. |
| 8 | Part 10 Bloomberg questions, heavy. **Mock 3.** | 8+ Bloomberg questions done; 10.0 patterns internalized. |
| 9 | Spaced review of everything; weak spots. **Mock 4.** | Every Part 10 page done at least once; flashcards green. |

"Ongoing" bars overlap on purpose - once you can, you should always have a case study or
Bloomberg question in flight alongside the reading.

## How to grade yourself

After each mock or solo attempt, score 0 to 2 on each line. Anything below 2 is next
week's homework.

- [ ] Scoped it: functional + non-functional with numbers, in about 5 minutes.
- [ ] Estimation done, and I stated what the numbers imply for the design.
- [ ] API and data model before internals.
- [ ] High-level design: every box justified, every arrow labeled, traced one read and
  one write.
- [ ] Went two levels deep on at least one component (mechanism + failure mode +
  recovery).
- [ ] Handled failure and scale explicitly.
- [ ] Stated the main trade-off and tied it to a requirement.
- [ ] Drove the conversation; took hints; never went silent; finished on time.

## Using the resources (Part 11)

Do not sit down and read *Designing Data-Intensive Applications* cover to cover before
starting - you will burn a month and forget most of it. Instead: when a note references
a chapter or paper, read that chapter *then*, with the design problem fresh in your
head. DDIA chapters 5, 6, 7, 9 map almost one-to-one onto Part 3, and that is the right
time to read them.

Papers (Dynamo, Kafka, Raft, Dapper, Spanner) are worth skimming for the problem they
solved and the one big idea - not the proofs.

## Common study mistakes

- **Reading instead of doing.** You cannot watch your way to this skill.
- **Not talking out loud.** The round is verbal. Practice verbal.
- **Collecting designs.** Memorizing "the Twitter design" fails the moment the
  interviewer changes one requirement. Learn the *building blocks* and the *process*.
- **Skipping estimation.** Without numbers your design choices are unmotivated, and
  interviewers notice immediately.
- **Never going deep.** Breadth-only reads as shallow. Pick something and go three
  questions deep.
- **Ignoring operations.** "What happens when it fails / at 10x / at 3am" is half the
  signal.
- **No mocks.** The gap between solo and live is large. Close it before the real loop.

## If you only have two weeks

Triage, in this order:

1. **Part 0** - the framework and estimation. Non-negotiable.
2. **Part 1** - CAP, consistency, ACID/BASE.
3. **Part 3.4 and 3.5** - sharding and replication. **Part 4.1 and 4.2** - caching and
   invalidation. **Part 5.1 and 5.2** - queues and delivery semantics. These four cover
   most of what you will actually use.
4. **Part 10** - do as many Bloomberg questions as you can, and read **10.0** twice.
5. **Two mocks minimum.**

Skip the deep theory (consensus internals, logical clocks) and come back to it only if a
mock exposes it.
