---
title: "0.2 The Bloomberg coding interview: the loop and what each round tests"
---

Everything on this page is drawn from candidate reports of the Oct-Dec 2025 new-grad and
internship loops. Where a claim comes from a single report, it says so. Processes change;
treat this as a well-informed prior, not a guarantee.

## The loop

| Round | Typical name | Length | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Phone screen / HackerRank-style | 45-60 min | Behavioural opener, then **one or two** coding questions, usually easy-to-medium. |
| 2 | Technical | 45-60 min | Behavioural, then one medium question, heavy on **follow-ups and edge cases**. |
| 3 | Design | 45-60 min | System design, **or** an OOP/class-design round, **or** a hybrid. |
| 4 | Engineering manager / team fit | 30-45 min | Behavioural, projects, team matching. |

Not every candidate sees all four, and the order of 3 and 4 varies. Several reports mention
a "loop stage" after the early rounds.

## Round 1 - the phone screen

**What it looks like.** A short behavioural opener - *"tell me about yourself"*, and in one
report *"is there anything on your resume you'd like to highlight"* - then straight into
code in a shared editor.

**Difficulty.** Easy to medium. The reported Round 1 questions include Two Sum variants,
Palindrome Number, Min Stack, Number of Islands, Decode String, Integer to Roman, Word Search,
Merge Intervals, and the recurring Collatz and invalid-transactions problems.

**What it tests.** Can you write correct code quickly while talking. That is nearly all.

**The trap.** Several Round 1 questions are *dressed-up* versions of standard problems -
the desert grid with fuel is BFS, the bank trading hours is merge intervals, the lottery
system is Insert Delete GetRandom. **Identify the underlying problem before you start
coding**, and say what you have identified.

**Speed matters more here than later.** One candidate finished the lottery question quickly
and the interviewer gave them a second problem "as a reward" - which is a good outcome, but
it means pace is being observed.

## Round 2 - the technical round

**What it looks like.** Longer behavioural section (one report says it took most of the
round), then one medium question worked in depth.

**What it tests.** Not whether you can solve it - whether you can be pushed. Every Round 2
report is dominated by **follow-ups**:

- Design Underground System, asked four separate times, and *every* report says the round
  was spent on edge cases: missed checkouts, overnight trips, intervals crossing midnight.
- Min Stack, followed by "top k minimums", "what about duplicates", and a pure Python probe:
  "what happens if you use `raise` instead of `return`".
- The CCTV top-N question, where the interviewer walked the candidate through two specific
  optimisations (min-heap instead of sort, binary search instead of scan) and then asked
  them to *justify why binary search was valid on that list*.
- The desert grid, where gas stations are added after you have solved it.

**The behaviour that scores.** Volunteer the edge cases before being asked. When you finish
the code, do not stop - say *"let me go through the edge cases"* and enumerate them. In this
round that is worth more than the code was.

## Round 3 - design

This round has **three different shapes** and you should be ready for all of them.

**(a) Classic system design.** The most reported by far. Bloomberg's questions are
recognisably Bloomberg: custom index, top-K news on the Terminal, market data fan-out,
portfolio with history, a note-taking app with offline sync, distributed logging. Full notes
for these live in the sibling system design course, Part 10.

**(b) OOP / low-level design.** Writing classes in an editor. One candidate: *"I was caught
totally off guard, it is not the typical system design question you'd expect."* The App Store
refactoring question (23.23) is the worked example. **This is the one that surprises people.**

**(c) A hybrid.** The anagram file-ingestion question (23.22) is a coding problem, a code
review of someone else's pseudocode, and a service design, with the interviewer explicitly
role-playing a colleague and mentor.

**How to handle the ambiguity.** Open by asking what kind of round it is: *"Would you like me
to focus on the distributed architecture, or on the class design?"* That question costs ten
seconds and saves you from answering the wrong one.

One report is a useful warning about (a): the interviewer *"did not want to hear anything
related to the server or the backend - he just wanted to see how the user would interact with
the system."* Follow the interviewer, not your template.

## Round 4 - engineering manager

Behavioural and team matching. Reports emphasise:

- Deep questions about **projects on your resume**. One candidate was asked repeatedly about
  specific programme experience and advises preparing those sections in advance.
- *"Give emphasis to the behavioural questions as much as the technical."*
- Ask questions back. One candidate reports asking three to six questions drawn from the
  interviewer's own introduction, and rates it as having gone well.

## What Bloomberg tests that other companies test less

**1. Edge cases, relentlessly.** This is the single strongest signal across all the reports.
Design Underground System was asked four times and the round was about edge cases every
time. Prepare a standard checklist (see 0.7) and run it out loud.

**2. Reading the requirement precisely.** Several questions are deliberately ambiguous:
- "at least one bank open" - one bank for the whole window, or a relay? (23.14, and the
  candidate misread it and ran out of time)
- "unique pairs" - unique by index or by value? (23.16)
- "characters in first but not second" - set or multiset? (23.21)
- "three or more consecutive" - remove the whole run, or three at a time? (23.5)

**Ask. Every time.** More candidates lost time to misreading than to not knowing an
algorithm.

**3. Language internals.** Asked verbatim in these loops:
- *"How does hashing work in a set and a hash map?"*
- *"What happens if you use `raise` instead of `return`?"*
- *"What are the drawbacks of the recursive solution?"*

See 0.6 for the answers. These are recall questions and you either have them or you do not.

**4. Follow-ups that change the problem.** Not "make it faster" but "now there are gas
stations", "now the number of people is odd", "now a process can have two parents". The
skill is recognising when a follow-up **invalidates your previous approach** and saying so.

## Practical notes from the reports

- **The interviewer may not volunteer a critical constraint.** In Two City Scheduling, the
  candidate was told *"the number of people could be odd, and the interviewer may not
  mention this at the beginning - you have to ask clarifying questions yourself."*
- **Interviewers are generally supportive.** Multiple reports describe them as helpful and
  willing to guide. Use that: think out loud so they *can* help.
- **If you realise your approach is wrong, say so immediately.** The trading-hours candidate
  realised at the end and had no time left. Announcing "I think I've misread this, may I
  restate it?" costs thirty seconds and saves the round.
- **Some questions come from a shared document** that circulates among candidates. Two
  reports mention being asked questions from such a doc. Part 23 is the equivalent, built
  from the reports themselves.

## Repeat rate

The strongest practical signal in the data: questions **repeat within weeks**.

| Question | Reports | Rounds |
|---|---|---|
| Invalid transactions | 6 | R1, R2 |
| Collatz 3n+1 | 6 | R1 |
| Design Underground System | 6 | R1, R2 |
| Desert grid with fuel | 5 | R1, R2 |
| Remove 3+ consecutive chars | 4 | R1, R2 |
| Lottery system (O(1) random) | 4 | R1, R2 |
| All paths between two nodes | 4 | R1, R2 |
| CCTV top-N in a time range | 3 | R2 |

If you prepare nothing else, prepare these eight. On the design side, **custom index** is
reported more than any other and has its own page in the system design course.
